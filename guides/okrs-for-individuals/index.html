    OKRs for Individuals: How to Use Them Without a Company Programme

    How one person can use Objectives and Key Results for a quarter: writing objectives that motivate, key results you can actually score, and the weekly check-in that makes the difference.

    OKRs were built for aligning companies, and most of the writing about them is about that. But the mechanism underneath — an inspiring objective, three to five measurable key results, scored honestly at the end of the quarter — works just as well for one person, with no software and no company programme. It is especially useful if your annual goals are vague and you want a way to turn them into a quarter of concrete progress.

    Grab the OKR worksheet; this guide explains how to fill it in well.

    Objectives: the "why" in one sentence

    An objective is qualitative, time-bound and slightly uncomfortable. It answers "what would make this quarter a success?" in a sentence you would be pleased to say out loud in December.

    Weak: "Improve my presentation skills." Strong: "Become the person leadership asks to present the team's work."

    Weak: "Get better at managing my time." Strong: "Own my calendar so that strategic work gets done every week, not just in a crisis."

    Limit yourself to two objectives per quarter as an individual, three at most. OKRs fail from dilution more than from anything else.

    Key results: the "how we'll know"

    Key results are numbers. Each objective gets three to five. If you cannot measure it, it is an initiative (something you will do), not a key result (something that will be true).

    For "Become the person leadership asks to present":

    • KR1: Deliver 4 presentations to audiences of 15+ this quarter (baseline: 1 last quarter).
    • KR2: Average feedback score of 4.3/5 or higher on a 3-question form after each (baseline: none — establish it).
    • KR3: Receive 2 unsolicited requests to present by quarter end (baseline: 0).

    Notice the baselines. Every KR should say where you are starting, or the score at the end means nothing.

    Initiatives: what you'll actually do

    Under each objective, list the actions that will move the KRs: "join the monthly all-hands speaker rotation", "record and review every presentation", "ask two senior people for one specific critique each". Initiatives can change mid-quarter; KRs should not.

    Scoring honestly

    At the end of the quarter, score each KR from 0.0 to 1.0. The convention that matters: 0.7 is a good outcome. If you consistently hit 1.0, your KRs were too safe. If you consistently score below 0.4, they were fantasy. The point of OKRs is calibrated ambition, and the score is the calibration signal.

    Then write two sentences per objective: what you learned, what you will change. This is the part most people skip and the part that makes the next quarter better.

    The weekly check-in

    OKRs die between the planning day and the scoring day. The fix is a ten-minute weekly check-in, ideally folded into a Friday weekly review:

    1. For each KR, note the current value.
    2. Rate your confidence (1–10) that you will hit it. Falling confidence is the early-warning signal; progress numbers alone lag.
    3. Pick one initiative to push next week.

    The worksheet has a twelve-week log for exactly this.

    Aligning with your manager

    You do not need your company to run OKRs for this to work, but your objectives should still map to something your manager cares about. Share the sheet at the start of the quarter: "These are my two focus areas this quarter; here's how I'll measure them." It gives your manager a clear picture of your priorities, produces the evidence for your self-evaluation automatically, and — if the objectives point at next-level work — builds your promotion case one quarter at a time.

    OKRs vs SMART goals

    Use OKRs when you want a quarter of directed ambition across several measures. Use a SMART goal when you have one specific, achievable target and a date. Many people run one or two OKRs for the quarter and write the individual key results as SMART statements — the two formats are complements, not rivals.
