    How to Get Promoted: The 12-Month Playbook

    A step-by-step plan for earning a promotion: decoding the bar for the next level, closing the gap visibly, building sponsors, and making the case so your manager can say yes in calibration.

    Promotions are not awarded for doing your current job well. They are awarded for already doing the next job, visibly, for long enough that not promoting you becomes awkward. That sounds unfair and it is also the rule almost everywhere, so the playbook below is organised around it: learn the bar, close the gap in public, build the coalition, make the case. Twelve months is realistic for one level in most organisations; six is possible when the gap is small and the sponsor is strong.

    Month 1: Decode the bar

    Get the written criteria for the next level — a career ladder, competency matrix or job description. If none exists, build one: ask your manager "What does someone at [next level] do that I don't yet?" and ask two people at that level how they got there. Write down the answers as observable behaviours, not adjectives. "Influences roadmap decisions across two teams" is a bar; "strategic thinking" is a vibe.

    Then rate yourself honestly against each criterion. The Performance Score Quiz is a reasonable first pass for the generic competencies; the role-specific ones need your manager's view. Ask directly: "If you were putting me forward today, which criteria would you struggle to evidence?"

    Month 2: Tell your manager the plan

    State the goal plainly: "I want to be at [level] by [date]. Here's what I think the gap is. Would you help me test that and close it?" This does three things. It makes your manager a co-author of the plan rather than a judge of it. It surfaces objections early, when they can be fixed. And it starts the clock on the "for long enough" requirement — most managers need to see next-level performance for two to four quarters before they will sponsor.

    Agree two or three gap-closing goals in writing using the SMART Goal Builder, each tied to a specific next-level criterion.

    Months 3–9: Close the gap where people can see it

    Take next-level scope before you have the title. Volunteer for the cross-team project, the customer escalation, the thing nobody owns. Scope is the currency of promotion.

    Make results legible. Keep a running achievements log — action, number, impact — and send a brief monthly summary to your manager. Nobody remembers March in December; your log does.

    Fix the one visible weakness. Every promotion case has a "but". Find yours in month 1 and remove it by month 6, then tell people it is gone: "You mentioned my updates were too long; here's the one-pager format I've used since April."

    Build sponsors, not just supporters. A supporter says nice things if asked. A sponsor argues for you in a room you are not in. You need at least one at the level above your manager. Earn it by solving a problem they care about and by making sure they see the result — a short note with the outcome is enough.

    Behave like the level. Bring options instead of problems. Write the summary before the meeting. Decide the things that are yours to decide. Managers promote people who are already making their lives easier at the next level.

    Month 10: Pre-wire the decision

    Promotions are decided in calibration meetings you will not attend. Your manager needs a case they can read aloud in ninety seconds. Write it for them: one page — the criteria for the level, your evidence for each, the sponsors who will vouch, the one weakness and how it was closed. Then ask: "Is there anything in here you couldn't defend? What would make this a certain yes?"

    Ask your sponsor, separately, whether they would support the case if asked. If they hesitate, you have found the real gap.

    Month 11–12: The formal ask

    In the review cycle, your self-evaluation should be written against the next level's criteria, not your current one, and the goals section should name the promotion explicitly. In the meeting, ask the direct question: "Am I being put forward? If not, what specifically is missing and when will we revisit?"

    If the answer is no

    Get the reason in writing as criteria, not adjectives, with a date for the next decision. If the reason is real, you now have a shorter list. If the reason is vague after two cycles, or the goalposts move, the honest conclusion is that this manager or organisation will not promote you, and the fastest promotion is often external. The same evidence log that failed to move your manager is exactly what a hiring manager wants to see — and the 30-60-90 day plan is how you walk into the new role already operating at level.
