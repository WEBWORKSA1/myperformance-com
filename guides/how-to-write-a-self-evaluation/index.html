    How to Write a Self-Evaluation That Gets You Promoted

    The 5-part structure managers actually read, 20 example sentences you can adapt, and the three mistakes that quietly cost people raises every review cycle.

    Your self-evaluation is the one document in the review process that you fully control. Your manager writes their assessment with your self-evaluation open in the next tab — and in calibration meetings, where ratings are compared across teams, the manager's write-up often borrows your sentences word for word. Write a vague self-evaluation and you hand the narrative to someone with thirty other things to do. Write a sharp one and you have effectively drafted your own review.

    This guide gives you the structure, the sentences, and the mistakes to avoid. If you want the structure done for you, use the Self-Evaluation Generator and come back here for the polish.

    Start with what the reader needs

    Before writing a word, answer three questions:

    1. What are the rating criteria? Get the competency list or rubric your company uses. Your evaluation should be organised so that a manager can map each paragraph to a criterion without effort.
    2. What does my manager already believe about me? Your evaluation should confirm the true positives, supply evidence for anything they may have missed, and pre-empt the negatives with your own honest framing.
    3. What do I want out of this cycle? A rating, a raise, a promotion, a change of scope. Every section should quietly point at it.

    The five-part structure

    1. Summary (2–3 sentences)

    Lead with your single biggest measurable outcome. Then name the theme of your period. Then signal growth.

    "This year I owned the migration of 40 enterprise accounts to the new platform, completing it two weeks early with zero churn. My focus was on execution under pressure and on making the process repeatable for the team. I also deliberately built my skills in executive communication."

    2. Key achievements (3–5 items)

    This section decides your rating. Use the pattern goal → action → result → impact, and put a number in every item. Numbers do not need to be revenue; time saved, error rates, adoption, satisfaction scores, headcount influenced, deadlines beaten and money protected all count.

    "Goal: reduce the support backlog. Action: introduced a triage rota and templated the 12 most common replies. Result: median first response fell from 26 hours to 5 (−81%). Impact: CSAT rose from 3.9 to 4.5 and two teammates were freed for project work."

    If you do not have numbers, estimate honestly and say so ("approximately", "based on ticket counts"). An honest estimate beats an adjective.

    3. Challenges and how you handled them (1–2 items)

    Choose something real. This section demonstrates judgement, and it lets you frame a known problem before your manager does. Structure: situation, what you did, outcome, lesson.

    "Q2's launch slipped by three weeks after a vendor dependency failed. I escalated on day two, negotiated a partial delivery, and re-sequenced the plan so marketing could proceed. We launched with 90% of scope and closed the gap by July. I now build vendor risk into every timeline."

    4. Strengths and development areas

    Two or three strengths, each anchored to an example already mentioned. Then one or two development areas, each with a concrete step you are taking. Self-awareness is itself a rated competency in many frameworks; a self-evaluation with no development areas reads as either dishonest or oblivious.

    "Development: I want to delegate more effectively. I have started handing the weekly status report to a junior colleague and am reviewing rather than writing it."

    5. Goals and support

    Two to four goals for the next period, each measurable and aligned to a team priority, followed by what you need from your manager. The support request matters: it turns the review into a two-way agreement and creates a record if the support is not provided.

    Twenty sentences you can adapt

    Results: "Delivered X ahead of the Y deadline, resulting in Z." · "Grew [metric] from A to B over [period] by [method]." · "Cut [cost/time] by N% through [change]." · "Closed [number] of [things], the highest on the team."

    Ownership: "When [problem] emerged I took the lead on [action] without being asked." · "I flagged [risk] early and proposed [mitigation], which avoided [consequence]."

    Collaboration: "Partnered with [team] to [outcome]; they cited [specific feedback]." · "Mentored [names/number], both of whom [result]."

    Communication: "Presented [topic] to [audience]; the recommendation was adopted." · "Introduced a weekly written update that reduced status meetings by N hours."

    Growth: "Completed [course/certification] and applied it to [project] within [timeframe]." · "Sought feedback on [skill] from [people] and changed [behaviour]."

    Challenges: "Faced [obstacle]; I responded by [action]; the outcome was [result]." · "I underestimated [thing]. I have since [change]."

    Development: "I want to strengthen [skill]. My plan is [action] by [date], measured by [signal]."

    Goals: "Achieve [metric] by [date] through [approach]." · "Own [scope] end-to-end by [quarter]."

    Support: "I would benefit from [specific resource] to accomplish [goal]."

    Close: "I welcome your feedback and look forward to discussing how these results align with the expectations for [next level]."

    Three mistakes that cost people raises

    Listing duties instead of results. "Responsible for client onboarding" tells the reader what your job description says. "Cut onboarding from 21 to 9 days" tells them what you did with it.

    Modesty by omission. Many strong performers leave out their best work because it "was a team effort" or "just my job". Credit the team and state your part precisely: "As the lead on the analysis workstream, I…"

    Burying the ask. If you want a promotion, say so in the goals section, in plain words, with the evidence above it: "I believe the results above meet the bar for [next title] and would like to discuss the path to it."

    Length, tone and timing

    One to two pages. Confident, specific, unemotional. Write the first draft two weeks before the deadline, leave it for three days, then cut 20%. Ask a trusted colleague to read it for anything that sounds like bragging without evidence — or evidence without a claim.
