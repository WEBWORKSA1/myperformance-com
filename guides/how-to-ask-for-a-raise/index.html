    How to Ask for a Raise: The Script, the Data and the Timing

    Exactly what to say when asking for a raise, the evidence to bring, when in the review cycle to ask, and how to handle the four most common responses — including 'no'.

    Most people ask for a raise at the wrong time, with the wrong argument, and accept the first answer. Fixing those three things does not guarantee a yes — budgets are real — but it changes the odds dramatically, and it changes what you get when the answer is "not now". Run your numbers through the Raise Calculator first: seeing the ten-year value of a five-point difference tends to cure any reluctance to have the conversation.

    When to ask

    Compensation decisions are usually made before the review meeting, often weeks before, when managers submit proposals for calibration and budget approval. Asking in the review meeting itself means asking after the money has been allocated.

    • Best: 6–8 weeks before annual reviews, in a scheduled 1:1, framed as "I'd like to talk about my compensation ahead of the review cycle."
    • Good: immediately after a visible win, a scope increase, or a strong project close.
    • Acceptable: any time, if you have been underpaid relative to market for more than six months.
    • Worst: in the review meeting; right after a company setback; by email.

    Find out when your company's cycle actually runs. Ask HR or a friendly manager: "When do comp proposals get submitted?"

    What to bring

    You need one of three cases, and ideally two.

    1. The performance case. Three to five quantified outcomes from the past year, in the same action → result → impact format as a self-evaluation. Show that your contribution grew faster than your pay.

    2. The market case. Three data points on what your role pays externally: salary surveys, published ranges on job postings (mandatory in several US states and in the EU from 2026), recruiter conversations. Present a range, not a single number.

    3. The scope case. Evidence that your responsibilities have expanded beyond your job description: people you now manage, budgets, systems, clients.

    Then decide your number. Ask for a specific percentage or amount at the top of the range you can justify. Managers rarely offer more than you ask; they often offer less.

    The script

    Open with the ask, not with a preamble about how uncomfortable this is.

    "I'd like to talk about my compensation. Based on my results this year and what the market pays for this role, I'm asking for an increase to [number], which is about [X]%. Let me walk you through how I got there."

    Then the evidence — two minutes, not ten:

    "Three things. First, [outcome with number]. Second, [outcome]. Third, [scope change]. On the market side, [source] puts this role at [range]; I'm currently at [position in range]."

    Then stop talking. Silence is your friend here.

    Close with a question that assumes movement:

    "What would it take to get there, and what's the timeline?"

    Handling the four common responses

    "There's no budget." Ask when budget is set and how you get into the next cycle. Then ask for something with no budget line: a title change, a scope change with a pay review in three months, extra leave, a training budget, or a written commitment. Get the date in writing.

    "You need to do X first." Excellent — this is a negotiation, not a refusal. Get X defined precisely, with a measurable bar and a date, and confirm that hitting it means the raise. Send a summary email the same day.

    "I'll see what I can do." Pin it down: "Thanks — when should I expect to hear back?" Follow up on that date.

    "Yes, but less than you asked." Don't accept on the spot. "I appreciate that. Can I have a day to think about it?" Then decide whether to accept, counter once, or accept with a follow-up review agreed for six months.

    What not to do

    Don't cite personal expenses — rent, kids, a mortgage. It is true and it is irrelevant to the business case, and it gives the manager an easy exit ("I understand things are tight"). Don't compare yourself to named colleagues. Don't threaten to leave unless you have an offer and are willing to take it. Don't apologise for asking.

    After the conversation

    Send a short email summarising what was agreed, with dates. Put the follow-up date in your calendar. Keep logging your achievements — the Self-Evaluation Generator works well as a running document — so the next conversation takes ten minutes to prepare instead of a weekend.

    If the answer was a firm no with no path, you have learned something valuable about your position in the organisation. The promotion playbook covers the next move, whether that is inside the company or outside it.
