    How to Give Feedback That Lands (Without the Sandwich)

    A four-sentence structure for feedback people actually act on, how to deliver critical feedback in a 1:1, what to say when someone gets defensive, and why the compliment sandwich fails.

    Managers give too little critical feedback, and what they give is often so cushioned that the recipient does not realise they received any. Then the annual review arrives with a rating that feels like an ambush. The fix is not harshness — it is structure, frequency and a clear separation between the behaviour and the person. This guide gives you a four-sentence model, a way to handle the hard moments, and a rhythm that makes review season uneventful.

    Why the compliment sandwich fails

    Praise–criticism–praise trains people to distrust praise and to miss the criticism. Research on feedback consistently finds that the message received is the one the recipient expected to hear; if the structure signals "this is a nice conversation", the critical middle is discounted. Separate the two. Praise specifically and often. Give critical feedback in its own moment, with its own clear frame.

    The four-sentence structure

    Built on the situation–behaviour–impact model, with a fourth sentence that most versions leave out.

    1. Situation. "In Tuesday's client call…"
    2. Behaviour. "…when Priya asked about the timeline, you said 'it'll be done when it's done'."
    3. Impact. "She followed up with me afterwards to ask whether the project was in trouble. It cost us some trust and cost me half an hour."
    4. Request or question. "Next time, can you give a date range and say what would move it? — Or is there something about that project's timeline I should know?"

    Sentence four turns feedback from a verdict into a conversation. Half the time it surfaces context you did not have.

    Everything in the first three sentences is observable and specific. No "you tend to", no "people say", no "attitude". If you cannot name the situation and the behaviour, you do not yet have feedback; you have an impression. Collect one more instance and then speak.

    Timing and setting

    • Within 48 hours of the behaviour. After a week it is history; after a month it is a grudge.
    • Privately, in a 1:1 or a short scheduled conversation. Never in group chat, never in a meeting.
    • Not on Friday afternoon, and not immediately before a high-stakes event for the person.
    • Ask first: "Can I share something I noticed?" It takes two seconds and changes how the next sentence is received.

    When they get defensive

    Defensiveness is normal and is not the same as refusal. Three moves:

    • Slow down and repeat the facts. "I'm not saying you're unreliable. I'm saying the report was due Tuesday and arrived Thursday, twice this month."
    • Acknowledge the context without dropping the point. "It sounds like the data feed was the problem. That's useful. And I still need to know by Monday if Tuesday is at risk."
    • End with the request, in writing if necessary. "Let's agree: a heads-up by Monday noon if the deadline is at risk. I'll note that in our 1:1 doc."

    Do not escalate the volume or extend the list. One behaviour per conversation.

    Making it routine

    Feedback lands better when it is frequent and unremarkable. Build it into the 1:1 rhythm:

    • Every 1:1 has a "feedback both ways" slot — the 1:1 Agenda Builder includes it by default.
    • Aim for a ratio of roughly four specific pieces of positive feedback to one critical, because that is what keeps critical feedback hearable — not because it is a formula to hit.
    • Keep a running note per person: date, situation, behaviour, impact, outcome. By review time you have twenty dated examples and the review write-up takes an hour.

    Asking for feedback as a manager

    Model the behaviour. Once a month ask each report: "What's one thing I could do differently that would make your work easier?" Then do it visibly and say so. Managers who do this get told the truth; managers who do not are told what they want to hear, and find out the rest in the engagement survey.

    Positive feedback deserves the same structure

    "Great job" is pleasant and useless. "In the vendor negotiation you asked for the breakdown before agreeing to the number, which saved us about $8k — do more of that" tells the person exactly what to repeat. Specific praise is also the most defensible evidence for the "exceeds expectations" ratings you will want to give later. The phrase library has the language; your notes have the examples.
