Companies track dozens of indicators to know whether they are performing. Most individuals track none, then discover how they are doing once a year, from someone else. That asymmetry is fixable in a spreadsheet and five minutes a week. Below are twelve indicators that predict how your performance will be judged, grouped into four categories. You do not need all twelve; the closing section suggests where to start.

Take the [Performance Score Quiz](/tools/performance-score/) first if you want a quick read on which category needs attention.

## Output

**1. Outcomes shipped per week.** Not tasks — outcomes: something finished that someone else can use. Count them every Friday. Trend matters more than the absolute number; a falling count with a rising to-do list is the earliest warning of overload.

**2. Commitment hit rate.** Of the things you said you would do by a date, what share did you deliver on time? Track promised vs delivered. Below 80% and people have quietly stopped planning around you; above 95% and you may be under-committing.

**3. Cycle time on your core deliverable.** For whatever you produce most — reports, features, proposals, tickets — how long from start to done? Shortening cycle time is usually the most visible improvement you can make in a quarter.

## Quality

**4. Rework rate.** What share of your deliverables came back for correction? Count the bounces. Anything above one in five suggests a self-review step is missing.

**5. Stakeholder satisfaction.** Once a quarter, ask the three people who depend most on your work one question: "On a scale of 1–10, how easy is it to work with me, and what would make it a 10?" Log the number and the answer.

**6. Defects or incidents attributable to your work.** Engineers count bugs; analysts count corrected figures; account managers count escalations. Zero is not the goal — an honest count that trends down is.

## Growth

**7. Feedback requested and acted on.** Count the times per month you explicitly asked for feedback and the times you changed something because of it. Two per month is a strong pace.

**8. Skill investment hours.** Hours per week spent deliberately learning something relevant to your role or the next one. Two to three hours sustained beats a week-long course once a year.

**9. Scope growth.** Every quarter, list responsibilities you hold now that you did not hold a year ago. If the list is empty for two consecutive quarters, you are stagnating whether or not your rating says so.

## Focus

**10. Deep-work hours per week.** Uninterrupted blocks of 60+ minutes on cognitively demanding work. The [Focus Timer](/tools/focus-timer/) tallies this automatically. Twelve hours a week is good; twenty is exceptional.

**11. Meeting hours per week.** Straight from your calendar. Watch the ratio of meeting hours to deep-work hours — above 1.5:1 and your calendar is running you.

**12. Habit consistency.** Completion rate of the two or three daily habits that drive everything else (planning the day, closing the inbox until a set time, logging an achievement). The [habit tracker](/tools/habit-tracker/) reports this as a weekly percentage.

## Setting it up

One spreadsheet, one row per week, twelve columns (or fewer). Fill it in during your [Friday weekly review](/guides/weekly-review-system/); with the tools above doing the counting, it takes under five minutes. After eight weeks you have trend lines. After a quarter you have the evidence section of your [self-evaluation](/tools/self-evaluation-generator/) written for you.

## Where to start

Three, not twelve. Pick one from each of the categories where you feel weakest:

- If your reviews say "great work but hard to plan around" — start with **commitment hit rate**.
- If you are busy but not progressing — start with **deep-work hours** and **outcomes shipped**.
- If you want a promotion — start with **scope growth** and **stakeholder satisfaction**.

Add more only when the first three are automatic. A metric you stop tracking in week three is worse than one you never started, because it teaches you that measurement does not work. It does — when the measuring costs less than the insight is worth.
